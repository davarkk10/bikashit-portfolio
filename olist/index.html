Model

Star schema on SQL views

  • Two fact tables at two grains: one row per delivered order, and one row per order × seller. 1.3% of orders have two sellers, so seller and lane numbers count pairs, not orders.
  • Dimensions: date, customer state and region, seller (with a fixed late-rate decile), first order per customer.
  • Disconnected tables drive the benchmark switch (last year or target), the metric switch, the Top N slicer and the reconciliation table.
DAX

90+ measures, written to be checked

  • Like-for-like last year: the comparison stops at the last date with data and shows n/a when last year has under 300 orders.
  • One chart, four metrics: a SWITCH measure with a dynamic format string (%, stars or days).
  • Checked against SQL: the reconciliation exposed a filter bug. A Top 5 table used ALLSELECTED, so “vs Brazil” compared each state with the other four. REMOVEFILTERS fixed it.

Problems found and how I handled them
  • 547 orders have 2+ reviews and 789 review IDs repeat. I keep the latest answered review per order, so no order is counted twice.
  • 8 orders are marked delivered but have no delivery date. Excluded from delivery timing.
  • One customer can have many customer IDs. Repeat-purchase work uses customer_unique_id (96,096 people), not customer_id.
  • Payments reconcile: for 98,416 of 98,665 paid orders, payments are within R$1 of items + freight.
  • Thin edges: Sep–Dec 2016 and Sep–Oct 2018 have very few orders, so trends use Jan 2017–Aug 2018 only.
  • Definition of late: delivered to the customer on a later calendar day than the estimated date shown at purchase.
03 / FINDINGS

Three answers from the SQL.

Finding 1 · Late delivery and reviews

Reviews collapse once an order is 4+ days late.

Orders that arrive early or on the day get ~9–11% bad reviews (1–2 stars). At 1–3 days late that triples to 32%. From 4 days late, two out of three reviews are bad, and beyond a week it is about 80%.

Customers whose first order arrived late also came back slightly less often: 2.33% vs 2.86% bought again later (a correlation; repeat buying is rare on Olist overall).

Finding 2 · When it happens

Lateness spikes in peak months, and bad reviews follow.

In normal months 3–6% of orders are late. After Black Friday (Nov 2017: 12.3%) and in Feb–Mar 2018 (14.0%, 18.7%) the late rate jumped, and the share of bad reviews rose with it to 17–21%.

The promise itself is padded: the median order arrives 12 days before the promised date. So the problem is not that promises are too tight everywhere; it is that capacity runs out in specific months and lanes.

Finding 3 · Where it happens

It is mostly the delivery leg, on a few long lanes.

When the seller handed the parcel to the carrier on time, orders were still late 5.3% of the time, and these make up 72% of all late orders. Late seller handover raises the risk to 20.7%, but explains only 28% of lateness.

Late rates are highest for customers in the Northeast and Rio de Janeiro. The single biggest lane, São Paulo → Rio (8,274 orders, 14.0% late), alone holds 17.7% of all late orders. The worst 10% of sellers (63 sellers) add another 15.6%.

04 / ACTIONS

What I would tell the logistics team.

Each recommendation comes with the number I would track to see whether it worked.

01

Start with São Paulo → Rio.

One lane holds 17.7% of late orders. Review the carrier on that lane first: compare carriers, add a second option, and check where parcels wait.

Track: SP → RJ late % (14.0% today, Brazil average 6.7%)

02

Plan peak capacity before it is needed.

Late rates tripled after Black Friday and in Feb–Mar 2018. Book extra carrier capacity before those weeks, and send customers a proactive delay message when a parcel misses its ship-by date, before it becomes 4+ days late.

Track: monthly late % kept under 6% in peak months

03

Set promise dates by lane, and coach the worst sellers.

Promises are padded by a median of 12 days, yet long Northeast lanes still miss. Set the promise per lane from actual delivery times. Separately, 63 sellers produce 15.6% of late orders: give them a ship-by scorecard.

Track: late % for the worst seller decile (16.2% today)

05 / LIMITS

What this analysis does not prove.

  • Correlation, not cause. Late orders get worse reviews, but some bad reviews may come from the product itself. A controlled test (e.g. proactive delay messages for half the late orders) would measure the true effect.
  • Repeat purchase is rare (under 3%), so the 2.33% vs 2.86% gap is small even though it is statistically clear (z ≈ 2.5).
  • Old, one-country data. Brazil, Sep 2016 – Oct 2018. Patterns are useful for method, not as current facts.
  • No carrier field. The dataset does not name the carrier, so “delivery leg” means the time after the seller's handover, not a named company.
  • Reviews are optional. Only orders with a review are in the review analysis (95,824 of 96,478 delivered).
